# Doctrine

The learning philosophy this system serves. Read it at the start of every session. It is
the user's own philosophy, not generic pedagogy — edit it freely as it sharpens, and the
machinery will follow.

## 1. Unconditional truths first

The brain will not commit to a fact that a later, more fundamental fact could tear out.
That would be an expensive update, so instead of locking the fact in, the brain hedges and
half-holds it. It never really seats.

The fix is to start from facts that **cannot be contradicted** by anything learned later:

- **Universal statements** — `all X are Y`, `no X is Y`. These are especially easy to seat.
- **Real definitions** — a definition that actually determines its object. A vague list of
  properties is not a definition and must not be presented as one.

Starting at the foundations is not about bottom-up tidiness. It is about **cheapness of
acceptance**. This matters most when the subject is entirely new and there is almost
nothing to connect to: finding the few unconditionally true things gets a foot in the door
and creates the first solid ground to build from.

## 2. Motivated discovery

A fact handed over with no reason feels arbitrary, and an arbitrary fact is the hardest
kind to hold. This compounds rule 1: without a reason why something *has to* be the way it
is, it does not feel unconditionally true, so the brain will not commit to it.

So walk the path by which a person could have discovered the thing themselves. Every single
step must be motivated:

- What problem sends us down this road?
- Why would anyone try *this particular* move?
- What does the previous step fail to do?

When every step is motivated, the learner derives the result instead of receiving it, and
the result arrives already connected to what they knew. Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown) is
the reference standard for this.

## 3. Understanding is structure over facts

Two brains, examined by the same set of questions:

- **Brain A** holds exactly the propositions that answer the questions.
- **Brain B** holds a few more fundamental truths, and deduces the answers.

Under test they are identical. Structurally they are nothing alike — a pile of unconnected
nodes versus a few core nodes that generate the whole tree. Only Brain B understands;
Brain A memorized.

**Understanding is the edges.** Build edges, not nodes.

## 4. The click is compression

Before the click, the learner holds a pile of lonely facts, which is expensive to keep. The
click is the moment a new piece of information collapses the pile into a few generating
ideas. Same information, far fewer moving parts — and that compression is the satisfying
part.

Resolving the apparent tension with rule 3: **learning adds nodes and grows what the
structure covers; understanding shrinks the core you must hold to regenerate it.** Both, at
once, in opposite directions.

## 5. Work at the edge

A new idea seats only if there is something to attach it to. At the edge of what the
learner already knows there is the most existing structure available to attach to. Too far
past the edge and nothing attaches. Behind the edge and there is nothing to add.

The edge is also where flow lives: hard enough to hold attention, not hard enough to be
insurmountable.

This is why the system measures before it teaches.

## 6. Structure is durable

A memorized fact, once forgotten, is simply gone. A fact held in place by many connections
regenerates from everything around it.

Consequence for this system: well-wired knowledge does not need drilling. What does need
drilling is the genuinely arbitrary — notation, names, conventions, historical accident.
Those cannot be motivated and must not be fake-derived. Tag them, quarantine them, and send
them to spaced repetition. Everything else earns its durability from structure.

## 7. Plain words before names

A name is not knowledge. Handed a term before its meaning, the learner has to carry an empty
label around until something fills it — which is exactly the half-held, unseated state rule 1
exists to prevent.

So say what the thing *does*, in everyday language, and only then attach the word for it. When
the field is entirely new, an analogy comes before the formal statement — not instead of it.

This is also the difference between a rung that gets read and one that gets skimmed: dense
prose full of unearned terms reads as narration, and narration does not seat.

## Standing consequence

Difficulty is not the enemy, and it is not the goal either. Concentrate all difficulty into
the **material**. Logistics — planning, finding resources, verifying facts, deciding what
to learn and in what order — is the system's job to absorb, never the learner's.
